# Melody

## Large-scale shape (melodic contour)

The overall path a melody traces. Six categories, consistent across the typologies in use,
including CantoCore's six-part scheme.

| Shape | Motion | Feel | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arch** | Rises to a peak, returns down | Completeness, arrival and return. The most common shape in tonal music and in folk song. | Opening phrase of Für Elise |
| **Inverted arch** | Falls, then rises back | Tension and release | Opening of Greensleeves |
| **Ascending** | Rises, little or no return | Building, lifting, unresolved | Over the Rainbow, the opening leap and the rising phrases after it |
| **Descending** | Falls throughout | Settling, release, resignation | Scarborough Fair |
| **Undulating (wave)** | Equal up and down, similar intervals both ways | Fluid, calm, rocking | Twinkle Twinkle Little Star |
| **Static (horizontal)** | Stays in a narrow band | Steadiness. Intensity has to come from elsewhere. | The melody in Ravel's Boléro |

**Two caveats.** Over the Rainbow gets categorised differently by different sources: an arch by
one reading, a descending ramp by another, on the grounds that the highest note arrives almost
immediately and the rest walks down to the tonic. Both are defensible, which means these are
descriptive tools rather than facts about songs. Researchers also disagree about the number of
categories, with proposals of four, six and ten, and a recent paper argues contours don't cluster
cleanly at all.

**Boléro is worth attention** if the harmony underneath isn't changing. It's a static melody over
an unchanging ostinato and its interest comes almost entirely from layering rather than from the
tune. Useful as a warning as much as a model: if the melody only works once the arrangement
arrives, it isn't carrying its own weight.

## Phrase structure

**AABA.** Say a line. Say it again slightly changed. Go somewhere completely different. Come back
to the first thing. This is the backbone of most of the American popular songbook and it's the
same shape as a limerick, where the third and fourth lines depart before the last returns.

The "somewhere completely different" section is what makes it work. Without a real departure,
AABA collapses into repetition.

## Motivic development

How one short idea carries a long stretch, especially over static or repeating harmony. Write a
short cell, then transform it rather than replacing it.

| Operation | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Sequence** | Restate the cell at a different pitch level, usually a step or third up or down |
| **Inversion** | Flip the intervals upside down: a rising third becomes a falling third |
| **Retrograde** | Play the cell backwards |
| **Augmentation** | Same notes, longer durations |
| **Diminution** | Same notes, shorter durations |
| **Fragmentation** | Use only part of the cell, often the last two or three notes |
| **Extension** | Add to the end of the cell and keep going |
| **Displacement** | Same cell, entered at a different point in the bar |

A four-pass version over a repeating progression: state the cell whole; sequence it up a step;
fragment it and repeat the tail; state it whole again, higher, and resolve.

## Making a line pop

Devices that tend to produce a hook rather than a serviceable tune.

- **Land on notes that aren't in the chord.** A melody built out of chord tones sounds like the
  chords. Hold a ninth, a fourth or a sixth and let the tension sit before it resolves. This is
  the single most common cause of a bland line over good harmony.
- **One pitch, moving harmony.** Hold a single note while the chords change underneath. One
  note, three functions, no new material.
- **Repeat with one thing changed.** State the phrase twice and alter one note the second time.
  Not a new idea, the same idea with a flaw in it. Most memorable hooks are built this way.
- **One high point, arriving late.** A single highest note, reached near the end and not
  repeated, gives a phrase a shape. Melodies that wander in a narrow band with no peak read as
  aimless.
- **Chromatic approach.** Arrive at a chord tone from a half step below or above. Used at the
  same place each time through, it becomes a signature.
- **Space in front.** Silence before a phrase. Continuous melodies have nowhere to land.
- **Rhythmic hook.** The rhythm alone can be the memorable part. Fluttering repeated notes, an
  anticipation ahead of the beat, a phrase that starts on an off-beat every time.
