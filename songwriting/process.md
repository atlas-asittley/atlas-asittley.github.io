# Process

Working rules. Each one earned its place by having worked, or by being worth a try.

## Choosing what to work on

- **Play the set from the top. Wherever you stop is the assignment.** If the next song doesn't
  exist, write its intro or first verse. If a second verse is missing, write that. The set tells
  you what to do, so there's no menu to face when you sit down.
- **Build the set in order, song by song.** Write one, learn it, move to the next. Rearranging
  later is allowed and expected.
- **Decide the next session in advance, including the alternative.** "Tomorrow, lyrics for song
  two; if I stall, finish the riff." An if-then decided the night before, so nothing is left to
  choose at the desk. Facing a menu at the start of a session is how a session gets lost.
- **Half an hour with no next part: shelve it and move on.** Record the fragment and what you
  tried first, so parking isn't abandoning.
- **Don't work only on one song.** It becomes too much. Moving forward in the set is part of
  sustaining the work, not a distraction from it.

## Composing

- **Harmonic skeleton first.** One chord progression isn't a song. Add one or two more parts,
  then melodies on top. Record options to the looper as you go so there are references to choose
  between. Assemble into one loop or two consecutive loops. Lyrics last, laid over the top to
  replace the melody.
- **When nothing arrives, generate on purpose.** Waiting until you hear the next part in your
  head works beautifully when something comes and leaves you with no move at all when nothing
  does. So play ten bad next-parts deliberately, record every one, and mine them the next day.
  **Don't run the validator inside the loop.**
- **Audition parts over a loop.** Keep part A looping and play candidate B parts over or after
  it. No need to learn a smooth join to evaluate, and a two-bar fragment doesn't have to be
  performance-quality to be judged. Only learn the transition for the one you keep.
- **Unfinished riffs are a parts bin, not a graveyard.** An old fragment may be the B section of
  a current song. If it feels forced, it isn't the one. Don't push it.
- **Cheapest contrast needs no new material.** Rotate the progression to start on a different
  chord, change the feel, or sit on one chord and let texture carry the section.
- **Don't echo the chorus in the bridge** if the chorus already appears often. It spends the
  chorus early and the return lands softer. A texture change does the contrast work on its own.
- **An unchanging progression makes the melody carry everything.** Four identical passes with
  nothing else moving is a lot of weight for one line. Either give the melody a large-scale shape
  and motivic development, or let something else move.

## Lyrics

- **Generate in dead time, polish in session time.** Raw lyrics can be thrown together anywhere
  with a melody in your head: the gym, the car, waiting. Honing happens in the session. This
  gives the slowest part of the job more total hours without spending session time on it.
- **Nonsense first.** Sing syllables that fit the melody to make raw material, pile it up, then
  mine the pile for keepers. Judge afterwards, never during.
- **Ship the version you have and revisit later.** Get it out, get the hour built, hone once
  there's an hour to hone.

## Learning and rehearsing

- **Sing and play together from the start**, one line or even one note at a time. Learning the
  layers separately and recombining them is harder. Pedantic is just the right chunk size.
- **Mechanics to automaticity.** Get the guitar and the pedal moves free so attention is
  available for words. Improvising lyrics is impossible while still thinking about what the hands
  are doing.
- **Rehearsing is also the diagnostic.** A weak lyric can be spotted on the page, but whether a
  chorus lands as a chorus is a performance question. Singing it twenty times tells you what to
  fix.
- **Nobody in the room knows these songs.** A forgotten line is a different line, not a mistake.

## Set design

- **Alternate expensive and cheap items.** Written songs cost a lot. Improvisational pieces cost
  almost nothing to write. Alternating them is what makes an hour achievable, and it stops the
  hour being twelve written songs.
- **Attention arc.** Open with something conventional so the room settles, then something they
  have never seen so they look up.
- **Design for the instrument you are.** The best ideas solve your own constraints rather than
  fighting them. A piece built on a live radio sample needs no lyrics. A piece whose drum part is
  one looped measure of spoken words has nothing to memorise.
